Clutch Certified Reliability Report

Based on 100,000+ Clutch-purchased vehicles · Published April 2026

This is Canada's largest proprietary look at used-car reliability, built on the physical inspection of over 100,000 vehicles since 2017. Every brand score in this report draws from four streams of first-party Clutch data: 210-point inspection outcomes, reconditioning records, 90-day warranty claims, and customer returns.

Every vehicle Clutch considers for retail sale is measured against our 210-point standard, and roughly 50% don't pass. Those cars are routed to wholesale auctions and sold to other dealers. The 50% that do pass are reconditioned, listed on clutch.ca, and tracked through delivery, returns, and post-sale warranty claims.

Nine years of that work tells us which used cars in Canada hold up best.

Key Takeaways

The five things worth remembering from this report

  • Japanese brands dominate the top of the index. Lexus leads at 9.70, followed by Subaru, Toyota, Honda, and Acura.
  • Hybrids outperform both gas and pure electric vehicles in the first six years of ownership; the advantage narrows after 7 years as battery cells age.
  • Premium price doesn't predict premium reliability. German luxury sits below the 25-brand average; Japanese luxury sweeps the top.
  • Rust is the #1 cause of Canadian inspection failure, beating engine and drivetrain issues.
  • Brand matters more than mileage. A Honda at 120,000 km clears Clutch's standard at the same rate as a Chevrolet at 60,000 km.

How does our rating system compare to leading automotive reliability rankings?

J.D. Power surveys new-car owners in their first 90 days of ownership, useful for what's coming off the lot but less helpful in the used market. Consumer Reports combines owner surveys with long-term tests, but its dataset skews heavily American. NHTSA and Transport Canada track recalls and federally documented defects, which captures safety issues but not everyday reliability. None of these sources see what we see: tens of thousands of Canadian used cars moving through a single inspection, reconditioning, and post-sale process.

What is Clutch Certified, and what does the reliability index measure?

Clutch Certified is the standard every car on clutch.ca clears: a 210-point inspection, reconditioning to standard, and a 10-day return window. The same operational process generates four pillars of first-party reliability data that feed the index in this report.

How the four pillars feed the index: 210-point inspection (30%) · Reconditioning (20%) · 90-day warranty claims (35%) · 10-day return policy (15%)

The 210-point inspection

Every vehicle Clutch buys is checked against 210 items across 9 systems before it can reach a customer. Since October 2017, that's covered more than 100,000 vehicles.

Roughly half of the used vehicles Clutch buys never reach a customer. Those cars are sold to other dealerships through wholesale auctions instead of being listed on clutch.ca. Vehicles that don't meet the Clutch Certified standard get sent to wholesale for various reasons: major mechanical issues, damage to multiple body panels, smoke or pet odours, branded titles, or vehicles 11+ years old or over 150,000 km. That filter alone removes the bottom of the used-car market before a single car is listed for sale.

Reconditioning

Vehicles that clear inspection enter reconditioning. That's our pre-sale repair process: mechanical work, cosmetic fixes, glass, and standard prep. Clutch invests over $2,000 per vehicle on average, combining parts, technician labour, and shop overhead. The mix of what each brand needs is one of the strongest indicators in our dataset.

The 10-day return policy

Once sold, the customer has 10 days to return the car for any reason. Some returns are about the car itself: a problem the customer caught in the first ten days that our pre-sale process missed. Those car-related returns feed the return dimension of the index.

Warranty claim data

The warranty claim data behind the index comes from Clutch's 90-day warranty product, which we've offered for years and which customers can extend at purchase. Every claim is categorized by system, so we can see exactly which brands fail in which ways after delivery.

Why is Lexus #1?

Lexus tops the index at 9.70 because its weakest pillar is still excellent. Every other top-tier brand has at least one pillar that drops to "good" rather than great: Subaru's inspection-pass rate, Honda's customer-return rate, Acura and MINI on warranty rates near the 25-brand average. Lexus is the only brand in the dataset where all four pillars sit firmly in the top quartile. The math: Lexus's worst pillar (warranty, in the top 20% of brands) would be a top pillar for most of its competitors. The combination, not any one pillar, is what produces the lead.

What do the top brands share?

Every top-tier brand has the same pattern: no weak pillar. They arrive at Clutch in cleaner condition, need less reconditioning, and generate fewer post-sale issues. No single problem area drags them down. Japanese brands collectively file 90-day warranty claims at roughly 30% below the 25-brand average, and at less than half the rate of the German luxury and Stellantis clusters at the bottom.

Which brands sit in the middle?

Above the average line: Nissan (8.71), Kia (8.56), Hyundai (8.55), and Mitsubishi (8.20). Chevrolet (7.99) sits right on it. Hyundai and Kia stand out here. Hyundai and Kia's old reputation for unreliable engines traces to the 2011-2019 Theta II 2.0L and 2.4L. Today's lineup runs different architecture.

Nissan's middle-of-the-index position carries its own legacy issue: CVT transmission problems affected most of Nissan's lineup from 2010 through 2019, with the worst concentration in 2013-2018 model years. Newer Nissan models on revised CVT designs perform better, but the historical wave is still present in the used market.

Just below average: Volkswagen (7.77), GMC (7.71), Audi (7.58), and Ford (7.14). Each has its own weak spot, not a systemic problem.

Which brands score lowest, and why?

Below the 25-brand average sit eleven brands. Two clusters tell most of the story.

Stellantis. Jeep (6.38), Dodge (6.65), and Ram (6.21) share a parent company and share a reliability profile: higher inspection failure rates at intake, higher repair costs, and a dominant pattern of rust, drivetrain wear, and body-system issues.

German luxury. BMW (6.63), Mercedes-Benz (7.27), and Audi (7.58) all score below the 25-brand average. The three share a profile: elevated engine, electrical, and cooling claims; higher pre-sale repair costs; and warranty rates that run roughly 2× the Japanese top tier.

Tesla (6.92), Infiniti (6.27), Buick (6.92), and Volvo (7.35) round out the below-average tier, each with its own profile.

Do all brands fail the same way?

No. Every brand has a distinct reliability profile with its own characteristic failure pattern.

Some patterns are intuitive. Hyundai and Kia's engine issues concentrate on specific engine families. Dodge and Ram's rust profiles track Canadian salt exposure and a truck-heavy product mix. Audi shows a triple threat of engine, drivetrain, and fluid leaks.

Some are counter-intuitive. Tesla's top issue isn't electronics or software, it's rust on early Model 3 panels. Subaru's biggest failure category is aftermarket modifications on enthusiast models (WRX, BRZ). Honda and Toyota show high rates of structural damage simply because they're popular enough that more of them arrive with prior-accident histories.

What the same-platform comparisons reveal

The premium-vs-mainstream gap shows up most clearly when you control for the platform. Take BMW X1 and MINI Countryman: same platform, same engine, same drivetrain. The X1 fails Clutch's standard at roughly twice the Countryman rate at 4-6 years old. The premium tax shows up in both higher failure frequency and higher cost per repair.

By contrast, the Infiniti QX50 and Nissan Rogue, also platform mates with the same engine, fail Clutch's standard at essentially identical rates. The QX50's premium tax shows up only as a higher per-item recon cost, not as more frequent failures. When the underlying engineering is shared, the data shows clearly whether the badge is buying you trim or buying you problems.

What's the #1 reason Canadian used cars fail inspection?

Rust is Canada's leading cause of used-car failure. Nearly one in two vehicles that fail our inspection show rust or corrosion (48.5%). That's more than engine failure (40.8%), and nearly double drivetrain issues (29.7%).

The issues are specific: perforated frames, rotted subframes, bubbled rocker panels, corroded wheel wells, and structural rust that can't be safely welded. These aren't cosmetic problems. A rusted-through frame rail is a safety hazard.

Rust doesn't hit every brand equally. Ram (64%), Dodge (62%), Chevrolet (56%), Subaru (56%), and Tesla (57%) show the highest rust rates among vehicles that fail our inspection. But every brand on the list has a meaningful rust problem. It's not a fleet issue or a luxury issue, it's a Canadian-environment issue. Road salt, freeze-thaw cycles, and a five-month winter season do more damage per kilometre than the mechanical systems themselves.

Lack of routine maintenance is the multiplier on most rust failures. A vehicle that gets washed regularly and has its underbody treated holds up much better than one that doesn't, even in the same climate. Maintenance habits matter as much as where a car was driven.

What this means for buyers: mileage is a poor proxy for rust. A low-mileage car from a salted-roads market can be structurally compromised in ways the odometer doesn't show. Underbody inspections, which Clutch performs on every vehicle, are often the deciding factor.

Does mileage predict reliability, or does brand matter more?

Brand is a stronger reliability predictor than mileage. A Honda with 120,000-150,000 km fails Clutch's standard at the same rate as a Chevrolet with 60,000-80,000 km. Honda at the highest mileage band lands at 43.8%; Chevrolet at half that mileage lands at 40.8%. Same outcome, roughly 80,000 km apart. The Honda stretches twice as far before reaching the same point on Clutch's filter.

Are hybrids more reliable than EVs or gas cars?

Hybrids are the most reliable powertrain in Canadian used cars in the early years of ownership, beating both gas and electric through the first six years on the road. At 0-3 years, the fail rate is 6.2% for hybrids, 11.0% for gasoline, and 14.8% for electric. At 4-6 years, the gap widens to 14.9% hybrid, 24.0% gasoline, 27.2% electric. The picture is more mixed once vehicles are 7-10 years old: hybrid battery cells age, and the gap to gas narrows.

The mechanism is straightforward. A hybrid combines a mature gas engine with battery assist that reduces engine load. Decades of internal-combustion engine (ICE) refinement plus less wear means hybrids age well. But they don't inherit the early-adopter quality issues that still show up on pure EVs.

Pure EVs underperform gasoline at both age bands, a finding that cuts against common perception. The simpler drivetrain and regenerative braking don't compensate for the manufacturing-quality issues that still affect pure-EV production: panel gaps, suspension components, and build-quality variance.

The hybrid advantage has an age window. By 7-10 years, hybrid battery cells age and the gap to gas narrows. Toyota RAV4 Hybrid at 7-10 years fails our standard at essentially the same rate as the gas RAV4. Battery degradation, particularly in cold-climate driving where the internal combustion engine carries more of the load, is the most likely driver. The strongest hybrid case in the used market is for buyers shopping the 0-6 year window.

What this means for buyers: if reliability is the priority and you're shopping used in the 0-6 year window, hybrids deserve more weight than the current new-EV conversation suggests. Toyota RAV4 Hybrid and Corolla Hybrid rank in the top 10 models overall.

Is Tesla less reliable than other EVs?

No, not overall. Tesla and other EVs fail Clutch's standard at similar rates: 14.9% vs 14.6% at 0-3 years, 28.0% vs 23.8% at 4-6 years. The Tesla story isn't how often a vehicle fails, it's where. Tesla outperforms other EVs on electronics, tech, and powertrain, the exact systems Tesla gets criticized for. Tesla's real weak point is physical build quality: suspension, brakes, and body panels.

To separate Tesla-specific issues from EV-architecture issues, we compared Tesla against the rest of the EV market in our intake: Nissan LEAF, Ford Mustang Mach-E, and other EVs.

Tesla's electrical and tech issues run well below the other-EV average. Tesla's powertrain issues are a different league entirely, a small fraction of the other-EV rate. Tesla's EV architecture works.

Where Tesla is dramatically worse is physical build. Brakes and suspension issues run well above the other-EV average. Body and glass issues (panel gaps, door alignment, trunk function, windshield issues) are even worse. Pre-sale repair cost for brakes and suspension alone is roughly 2× the other-EV average.

These aren't software bugs. They're manufacturing consistency issues, the kind that show up on early Model 3 and Model Y production years and that NHTSA has documented in multiple suspension-related recalls.

The biggest Tesla-specific repair pattern shows up in suspension. Control arms and other suspension components frequently need premature replacement, with parts and labour typically running $1,000 to $2,000 per affected vehicle. The most likely drivers are excessive torque from the dual-motor instant-acceleration powertrain, the curb weight that comes with a battery pack, and harder driving habits among Tesla owners than the average gas-vehicle owner.

Teslas also arrive at Clutch needing tire reconditioning more often than mainstream brands, and the tires themselves cost more to replace. Higher curb weight, instant torque, and larger performance-spec wheels all wear tires faster than equivalent gas vehicles.

What this means for buyers: Tesla's tech reputation is overstated in the negative. The real watch-outs are physical build: panel gaps, door latches, suspension, HVAC. Our 210-point inspection catches most of these. Any Tesla buyer should still scrutinize the bodywork carefully and ask about prior recall service.

Conclusion

Nine years and 100,000+ inspected used cars give the Canadian market its first proprietary reliability picture, built on physical inspection and post-sale outcomes rather than owner surveys.

The patterns are clear: Japanese brands sweep the top of the index, German luxury and Stellantis cluster at the bottom, hybrids outperform both gas and EVs in the early years of ownership, and brand matters more than mileage at every age band.

But every car Clutch sells has already cleared the 210-point standard, been reconditioned to spec, and is backed by our 10-day return policy. The process raises the average reliability score across all 25 brands from 7.99 to 9.32. That's a +1.33-point lift. A Clutch Certified car is measurably more reliable than the brand it belongs to, because of the $2,000+ of reconditioning that goes into every vehicle.

How is the Clutch Certified Reliability Index calculated?

The index blends four pillars into a single composite reliability score for each brand. Warranty claim activity carries the largest weight (35%) because it measures what actually breaks after the car reaches a customer. Pre-sale pillars (210-point inspection and reconditioning) and post-sale pillars (90-day warranty claims and 10-day returns) are balanced 50/50.

The warranty claim data comes from Clutch's 90-day warranty product, which we've offered for years and which customers can extend at purchase.

All measurements are normalized within age buckets (0-3, 4-6, and 7-10 years) so older vehicles aren't penalized for their age, then volume-weighted across a brand's retail population. A brand must have meaningful volume in at least two age buckets to be rated. Twenty-five brands meet that threshold.

Full methodology, data sources, and calculations are published in a companion document available to media and research partners on request.
