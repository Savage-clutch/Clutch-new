This is Canada's largest proprietary look at used-car reliability, built on the physical inspection of over 100,000 vehicles since 2017. Every brand score in this report draws from four streams of first-party Clutch data: 210-point inspection outcomes, reconditioning records, 90-day warranty claims, and customer returns.

Every vehicle Clutch considers for retail sale is measured against our 210-point standard, and roughly 50% don't pass. Those cars are routed to wholesale auctions. The 50% that pass are reconditioned, listed on clutch.ca, and tracked through delivery, returns, and post-sale warranty claims. Nine years of that work tells us which used cars in Canada hold up best.

How does our rating system compare to leading automotive reliability rankings?

J.D. Power surveys new-car owners in their first 90 days of ownership, useful for what's coming off the lot but less helpful in the used market. Consumer Reports combines owner surveys with long-term tests, but its dataset skews heavily American. NHTSA and Transport Canada track recalls and federally documented defects, which captures safety issues but not everyday reliability. None of these sources see what we see: tens of thousands of Canadian used cars moving through a single inspection, reconditioning, and post-sale process.

What is Clutch Certified, and what does the reliability index measure?

Clutch Certified is the standard every car on clutch.ca clears: a 210-point inspection, reconditioning to standard, and a 10-day return window. The same operational process generates four pillars of first-party reliability data that feed the index in this report.

Every vehicle Clutch buys is checked against 210 items across 9 systems. Vehicles that clear inspection enter reconditioning — mechanical work, cosmetic fixes, glass, and standard prep. The mix of what each brand needs in reconditioning is one of the strongest reliability signals in the dataset.

Roughly half of the vehicles Clutch buys never reach a customer. Those are sent to wholesale auctions: major mechanical issues, multi-panel damage, smoke or pet odours, branded titles, or vehicles 11+ years old or over 150,000 km.

Once sold, the customer has 10 days to return the car for any reason. Car-related returns feed the return dimension of the index. The 90-day warranty product provides the claim data — every claim categorized by system, so we can see exactly which brands fail in which ways.

What the same-platform comparisons reveal

The premium-vs-mainstream gap shows up most clearly when you control for the platform. Take BMW X1 and MINI Countryman: same platform, same engine, same drivetrain. The X1 fails Clutch's standard at roughly twice the Countryman rate at 4–6 years old. The premium tax shows up in both higher failure frequency and higher cost per repair.

By contrast, the Infiniti QX50 and Nissan Rogue, also platform mates with the same engine, fail Clutch's standard at essentially identical rates. The QX50's premium tax shows up only as a higher per-item recon cost, not as more frequent failures. When the underlying engineering is shared, the data shows clearly whether the badge is buying you trim or buying you problems.

05 — What Fails

What's the #1 reason Canadian used cars fail inspection?

Rust is Canada's leading cause of used-car failure. Nearly one in two vehicles that fail our inspection show rust or corrosion (48.5%). That's more than engine failure (40.8%), and nearly double drivetrain issues (29.7%).

Every brand on the list has a meaningful rust problem — it's a Canadian-environment issue, not a brand issue.

The issues are specific: perforated frames, rotted subframes, bubbled rocker panels, corroded wheel wells, and structural rust that can't be safely welded. These aren't cosmetic problems. A rusted-through frame rail is a safety hazard.

Road salt, freeze-thaw cycles, and a five-month winter season do more damage per kilometre than the mechanical systems themselves. No brand is immune — the Canadian climate is the variable, not the badge on the hood.

What this means for buyers: mileage is a poor proxy for rust. A low-mileage car from a salted-roads market can be structurally compromised in ways the odometer doesn't show. Underbody inspections — which Clutch performs on every vehicle — are often the deciding factor.

06 — Mileage vs Brand

Does mileage predict reliability, or does brand matter more?

Brand is a stronger reliability predictor than mileage. A Honda with 120,000–150,000 km fails Clutch's standard at the same rate as a Chevrolet with 60,000–80,000 km. The Honda stretches twice as far before reaching the same point on Clutch's filter.

By 7–10 years, hybrid battery cells age and the gap to gasoline narrows. Toyota RAV4 Hybrid at 7–10 years fails our standard at essentially the same rate as the gas RAV4. The strongest hybrid case in the used market is squarely in the 0–6 year window.

What this means for buyers: if reliability is the priority and you're shopping used in the 0–6 year window, hybrids deserve more weight than the new-EV conversation suggests. Toyota RAV4 Hybrid and Corolla Hybrid rank in the top 10 models overall in this dataset.

09 — Tesla & EVs

Is Tesla less reliable than other EVs?

No, not overall. Tesla and other EVs fail Clutch's standard at similar rates: 14.9% vs 14.6% at 0–3 years, 28.0% vs 23.8% at 4–6 years. The Tesla story isn't how often a vehicle fails, it's where. Tesla outperforms other EVs on electronics, tech, and powertrain. Tesla's real weak point is physical build quality: suspension, brakes, and body panels.

The verdict: Tesla's overall fail rate sits within a few percentage points of the broader EV market. What separates Tesla isn't how often a vehicle fails — it's where. Tesla matches or beats other EVs on powertrain, electrical, and tech. Tesla runs 2–3× worse on brakes, suspension, body, and glass. The reliability pattern is build, not battery.
Tesla's EV architecture works. Tesla's factory doesn't.

Powertrain and electrical issues run well below the other-EV average. Where Tesla is dramatically worse is physical build: brakes, suspension, panel gaps, door alignment, and glass. Pre-sale repair cost for brakes and suspension alone runs roughly 2× the other-EV average.

The biggest Tesla-specific pattern is suspension — control arms and components that frequently need premature replacement, typically $1,000–$2,000 per affected vehicle.

What this means for buyers: Tesla's tech reliability is underrated. The real watch-outs are physical build quality: panel gaps, door latches, suspension, HVAC. Our 210-point inspection catches most of these, but any Tesla buyer should scrutinize the bodywork and confirm prior recall service.

Conclusion

Nine years and 100,000+ inspected used cars give the Canadian market its first proprietary reliability picture, built on physical inspection and post-sale outcomes rather than owner surveys.

The patterns are clear: Japanese brands sweep the top of the index, German luxury and Stellantis cluster at the bottom, hybrids outperform both gas and EVs in the early years of ownership, and brand matters more than mileage at every age band.

How is the Clutch Certified Reliability Index calculated?

The index blends four pillars into a single composite reliability score for each brand. Warranty claim activity carries the largest weight (35%) because it measures what actually breaks after the car reaches a customer. Pre-sale pillars and post-sale pillars are balanced 50/50.

All measurements are normalized within age buckets (0–3, 4–6, and 7–10 years) so older vehicles aren't penalized for their age, then volume-weighted across a brand's retail population. A brand must have meaningful volume in at least two age buckets to be rated. Twenty-five brands meet that threshold.

Full methodology, data sources, and calculations are published in a companion document available to media and research partners on request.
